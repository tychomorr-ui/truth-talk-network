# Cryptographic key control versus real-world identity

## Findings

NIST’s current final guidance is SP 800-63-4, published July 31, 2025. It treats identity proofing, authentication, and federation as distinct functions with distinct assurance levels (IAL, AAL, and FAL). NIST describes IAL as the robustness of proofing an individual’s identity, while AAL concerns authentication strength and the binding between an authenticator and a specific individual’s identifier. A cryptographic signature or successful key operation is therefore not, by itself, an identity-proofing result. SP 800-63-4 is guidance for users interacting with government information systems over networks; it is a useful benchmark here, not proof that a social client is NIST-conformant or certified. [1] [2]

NIST defines identity proofing as establishing, to a stated level of confidence, that the applicant is who they claim to be. Its process separates identity resolution (is this a unique, real-life identity in the population?), evidence and attribute validation (are the evidence and attributes genuine and accurate?), and verification (is the applicant the person to whom that evidence belongs?). Successful proofing ordinarily leads a credential service provider (CSP) to establish a subscriber account and bind one or more authenticators to that proven identity. NIST’s IAL requirements call for evidence, core attributes and their validation, and verification of the applicant’s ownership of evidence; IAL2 adds requirements intended to better mitigate impersonation and proofing errors than IAL1. A keypair with no such proofing and binding has no NIST IAL merely because it signs posts. [3] [4]

NIST defines authentication more narrowly: a verifier checks a claimant’s possession and control of an authenticator using an authentication protocol, confirms the authenticator is bound to the subscriber account, and checks that the account is active. AAL1 already requires proof of possession and control of the authenticator bound to an account; AAL2 and AAL3 impose stronger authenticator/factor requirements. This proves a claimant can use an authenticator in that account context. It does not independently show that the current claimant is the human named by a public key or that the person is the original proofed subscriber. NIST explicitly warns that technical controls generally cannot detect or prevent a subscriber’s willful credential sharing or collusion. [2] [5]

NIP-01 specifies a Nostr event’s `id` as SHA-256 of a canonical serialization of the event fields and `sig` as a Schnorr signature of that digest under the event’s public key. A successful validation shows the event data matches the signed digest and that the signature verifies under the included public key. It ties the event to a key. It does not establish the key’s civil identity, who had access to the private key when it was used, whether it was shared, stolen, compromised, or operated by software, or whether statements or media in the event are true. For uploaded media held outside the signed event, the signature covers only what the event actually contains—such as a URL or a separately recorded media digest—not bytes that are neither included nor cryptographically bound into the event. [6]

A SHA-256 digest is a compact digest of specified bytes. NIST says the Secure Hash Standard digests can be used to detect whether a message has changed since the digest was generated. If a receipt securely commits to a digest and the supplied file recomputes to that digest, this supports a byte-for-byte integrity comparison with the committed object. A bare hash does not identify who created, uploaded, possessed, or reviewed the bytes; prove that their content is true or lawful; prove when they existed; establish chain of custody; or guarantee that a copy remains available or has been erased. The anchoring mechanism must itself be described: a hash inside a Nostr event is key-attributed according to that event’s signature, while a separately trusted timestamp token can support proof that the hashed datum existed before a time. [7] [8] [6]

Nostr’s `created_at` is an event field supplied in the event serialization; a valid event signature binds that asserted timestamp to the event but does not make the timestamp an independent trusted clock. RFC 3161 describes a different mechanism: a Time-Stamp Authority (TSA), operating under an identified policy and trustworthy-time requirements, signs a token supporting proof that a datum existed before a stated time. This proves neither authorship nor identity of the person requesting the timestamp. [6] [8]

A C2PA manifest packages assertions and a claim with a cryptographic signature and content binding. Validation can support that the manifest is well-formed and that signed claims and covered asset portions have not been altered since signing; credential validation may also support trust in the signer under the validator’s trust model. C2PA’s own specification says the consumer uses the signer’s identity and other signals to decide whether assertions are true. C2PA’s guiding principles say the standard verifies association/integrity of assertions, not whether provenance is “good” or “bad,” and does not require the person or organization making a claim to be identified. Therefore C2PA is provenance evidence, not standalone proof of real-world human identity, truth of depicted events, or completeness of media history. A missing credential is not proof of manipulation; a valid one is not proof that an unrecorded claim is true. [9] [10]

NIST SP 800-63A recognizes forged media and injection as material identity-proofing threats. It notes that all remote proofing types are vulnerable; a biometric comparison on a captured sample alone does not prevent these attacks, and even live-capture and presentation-attack controls are not sufficient for every case. A user-uploaded screenshot, screen recording, video, document, or file—whether or not it carries C2PA metadata—should not be treated as proof that the named poster is the depicted person or that the represented event happened as described. [11] [9]

Deletion and moderation are separate from cryptographic authenticity. Under NIP-09, deletion is an author-signed request; relays “SHOULD” delete or stop publishing referenced events, and clients “SHOULD” hide or indicate them. The NIP expressly allows a client to warn that deletion cannot be guaranteed across all relays and clients. A local static client can hide content in its own interface, but it cannot erase other relay copies or copies already fetched, and an event’s signature/hash cannot revoke or retract its bytes. NIP-01 describes relays as endpoints that accept, reject, store/return events and apply their own limits; it does not grant one client global control over them. [12] [6]

## Product constraints for Truth-Talk / 4Reels

- Label the states separately. “Signed by key `…`” or “signature valid” is defensible after local verification. Do not translate it into “verified person,” “authentic identity,” “real creator,” or “anti-catfish verified” absent a separate, disclosed identity-proofing and key-binding basis.
- Keep media receipts narrow and explicit. Compute and retain a digest for each exact uploaded byte object; state whether a receipt covers an original, a transcode, an edit, a screenshot, or only a link. Verify the digest on retrieval. Do not call the digest proof of authorship, time, truth, ownership, consent, safety, legality, or deletion. A Nostr `created_at` is a signed claim, not an independent timestamp; a stronger time claim needs a separately validated trusted timestamp mechanism and its trust policy.
- Verify Nostr event IDs and signatures before displaying a “valid signature” state. Make clear that the signer is the event key, not necessarily the poster as a natural person; key loss, compromise, delegation, sharing, and recovery change the meaning of control. NIP-01’s cryptography alone cannot resolve those conditions.
- If “verified identity” is a product requirement, require a distinct identity-proofing design: identify the responsible CSP/issuer, evidence and verification path, assurance scope, binding of proofed account to key, key replacement/revocation, privacy/data-retention terms, fraud handling, and user recourse. Alternatively, a future integration could consume a properly authenticated external identity assertion and validate its issuer/trust context. A static client with no approved server/database does not itself provide NIST-style CSP enrollment, maintained account-to-authenticator binding records, fraud investigation, or account recovery; do not claim it does.
- Treat C2PA as an optional, narrowly scoped provenance signal. Validate the manifest, signature, certificate chain/trust status, and which asset bytes are covered; explain who/what the credential identifies (if ascertainable) and distinguish created from gathered assertions. Do not make C2PA presence mandatory as a truth test or absence an automatic deception verdict.
- Separate local user controls from network-wide enforcement. A client may locally label, filter, block, or hide. It cannot promise platform-wide removal or moderation of Nostr content without cooperation and policy mechanisms at the relays and other clients. NIP-09 is a request-based mechanism, not a deletion guarantee. If public-feed policy compliance requires enforceable global takedown, reliable abuse intake, repeat-offender controls, or durable audit, the architecture needs an explicitly approved enforcement/operations layer; it is not supplied by hashes, signatures, or a static client.
- NIST’s anti-forgery guidance is for identity-proofing processes, not a certification for uploaded UGC moderation. Do not claim that document scans, selfies, liveness checks, C2PA, or event signatures eliminate catfishing, deepfakes, or impersonation; communicate residual uncertainty and make any specific verification action and its limitations visible.

## Uncertainty and qualification

NIP-01 and NIP-09 are protocol documents in the Nostr NIPs repository marked respectively “draft/mandatory/relay” and “draft/optional/relay”; their text specifies SHOULD-level relay behavior, not universal deployment or governance. Actual relay storage, filtering, retention, and deletion vary. No product-specific policy taxonomy, identity-proofing vendor, trust anchor, timestamp authority, or enforcement service is approved in the scoped facts, so none is assumed here. C2PA trust is conditional on the credential and the validator’s trust configuration; C2PA itself does not assert the truth of content. NIST SP 800-63-4 is the current final revision found, published July 2025; its implementation guidance should be checked again when implementation is approved. [1] [6] [9] [10] [12]
