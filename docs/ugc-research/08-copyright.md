# Copyright and notice handling for user content

Section 512 of the Copyright Act establishes conditional liability limitations (safe harbors), not a general declaration that user uploads are lawful and not a guarantee that a platform cannot be sued. Hosting user-directed content is one covered service-provider function. For that function, the statute conditions the subsection (c) limitation on, among other things, expeditious action on qualifying notices, handling actual or apparent knowledge, and designating an agent; Section 512(i) also requires a reasonably implemented repeat-infringer policy and accommodation of qualifying standard technical measures. The Copyright Office describes four distinct covered functions—conduit, caching, hosting, and search/linking—with differing conditions. Whether a static Nostr client, any operator of relays, a file host, or a combination falls within a category or qualifies for a safe harbor depends on the actual service and facts; these sources do not classify Truth-Talk’s proposed architecture. [1] [2]

## Notice, removal, counter-notice

For a hosting provider relying on Section 512(c), a notice must be a written communication to its designated agent and substantially include: the complaining person’s physical or electronic signature; identification of the copyrighted work (or a representative list for multiple works on one site); identification of the allegedly infringing material and enough information to locate it; contact information; a good-faith belief statement that the use is unauthorized by the owner, agent, or law; and an accuracy statement plus a statement under penalty of perjury that the sender is authorized to act for the owner. A compliant notice calls for expeditious removal or disabling of access to the identified material on the provider’s system/network. A missing element does not automatically count as statutory notice, though the statute provides a prompt-contact/reasonable-assistance rule for certain incomplete notices. The notice process is about claimed infringement; a notice does not itself adjudicate ownership, infringement, fair use, or liability. [1] [2]

The provider must take reasonable steps promptly to tell the uploader of a Section 512(c) removal. A subscriber may send a counter-notice to the designated agent. It includes a physical/electronic signature, identification and former location of the removed item, a good-faith-under-penalty-of-perjury statement that removal resulted from mistake or misidentification, the subscriber’s name, address and telephone number, consent to the specified federal court jurisdiction, and agreement to accept service of process. The provider sends the counter-notice to the original complainant and states it will restore access; it must restore no earlier than 10 and no later than 14 business days after receiving it unless the designated agent first receives notice that the complainant filed an action seeking a court order. This is a process and timetable, not a ruling on the merits. [1] [2]

Knowingly materially misrepresenting that material is infringing, or that it was removed by mistake, can create liability for damages, costs, and attorneys’ fees under Section 512(f). Section 512(g) generally limits liability for good-faith removal, subject to its statutory conditions. A product workflow should capture the required statements and route notices and counter-notices to an accountable human process; hashes, automated similarity matches, policy reports, or identity scores should not silently decide copyright ownership or fair use. [1]

## Agent and operating constraints

A provider seeking Section 512(c) protection must publish its agent’s contact information in a publicly accessible place on its service and provide it to the Copyright Office. The current Copyright Office rule also requires accurate and current information and electronic designation; the designation expires after three years unless amended or resubmitted to renew it. Required public details include the provider’s legal name and physical street address (with a narrow waiver process), alternate names such as website/app names, and agent contact details. These requirements entail an identifiable operator and continuing maintenance; an anonymous, unstaffed client with no approved server/database has not demonstrated this capacity. Do not represent that “decentralized,” static, or signed architecture by itself supplies a compliant designated-agent operation. [1] [3]

Section 512(i)’s repeat-infringer condition requires an adopted and reasonably implemented termination policy and informing subscribers/account holders of it. Section 512 does not require affirmative monitoring to qualify for the safe harbors, and the Copyright Office distinguishes notice handling from a duty to search the service for infringement. That is not permission to ignore knowledge or notices. Product decisions about moderation, disabling access, and account/event treatment must account for the actual service operator’s ability to act, and should not be marketed as a legal conclusion. [1] [2]

A decentralized feed creates an operational mismatch: Section 512’s notice/removal and counter-notice sequence assumes a provider can identify and remove or disable access to material on its system or network, notify the uploader, accept a counter-notice, and restore access on the statutory schedule when applicable. A client-side hide/block action changes what that client displays; it does not remove an event from relays or delete downloaded copies. A relay or storage operator may have separate control of its copy, but one operator cannot promise universal removal from independent relays, archives, peers, screenshots, caches, or devices. Build any future notice flow around stable content location identifiers, operator-specific scope, receipt and deadline records, notices to affected users, counters, and explicit limits on global deletion; do not claim this research establishes which parties legally qualify as providers. [1] [4] [5]

## What the integrity and identity mechanisms establish

- **SHA-256 hash / hash-anchored receipt.** NIST describes secure-hash digests as a way to detect whether a message has changed since the digest was generated. For a specified byte sequence and implementation, a matching SHA-256 digest is evidence that the compared bytes match, subject to the cryptographic algorithm’s assumptions. An external timestamp or append-only anchor may show that a particular digest was recorded by that system at a particular point, to the extent the anchor and timestamp are trustworthy. Neither the digest nor the anchor alone establishes who made or possessed the file, when the underlying file was first created, copyright ownership, permission, fair use, authenticity of a screenshot/video, or legality. A digest is not a searchable location or a deletion command. [6]
- **Nostr event signature.** NIP-01 defines an event ID as SHA-256 of a specified serialization of event fields, and the event signature as a signature over that hash using the event key. Verification can establish that the event data matches that signature and that the corresponding private key signed it; if the event includes an attachment hash, the signature binds that reference, not the external file unless a verifier independently hashes the exact file and confirms the match. It does not independently establish a real-world person’s identity, that the person is the legal author or rights holder, authority to license/reproduce incorporated works, truth of the post, or lawful use. A signed event’s `created_at` is an event field, not by itself an independent trusted timestamp. [4] [7]
- **C2PA Content Credential.** A valid, trusted C2PA manifest can cryptographically bind specified provenance assertions to an asset and associate the signed claim with a signing credential; validation can report whether covered asset portions have changed since the manifest was produced. The C2PA specification expressly does not judge whether assertions are good/bad or whether they are true: people consuming the content assess their truth. A credential identifies a signer under C2PA’s trust model, not necessarily the human shown in a clip, copyright owner, license status, or legal authenticity. Credentials may be absent, removed, or not verifiable. It is a provenance signal, not a substitute for moderation or identity verification. [8]
- **Identity proofing.** NIST’s identity-proofing framework aims to associate an applicant with a real-life person to a stated assurance level, including validation of identity evidence and verification that the applicant is its owner. The strength depends on the chosen assurance level and process. It can raise confidence about who enrolled or controls an account; it does not prove copyright ownership, authorship of a particular upload, truthfulness, consent of people depicted, or absence of impersonation in every post/video. A keypair, account badge, or C2PA signer credential without such proofing is not proof of civil identity. [9]
- **Deletion and moderation.** NIP-09 is an optional, draft relay protocol: a kind-5 event asks relays to delete/stop publishing referenced events, while clients should hide or indicate them; relays only “SHOULD” comply, and the specification warns deletion cannot reach every relay/client. Client moderation can suppress presentation within that client’s own interface, but cannot recall bytes already copied elsewhere. Neither is equivalent to erasure from every network participant or archival location. [5]

## Implementation constraints for the proposed product

1. Do not launch or claim a Section 512(c) notice-and-takedown process unless the accountable service operator can designate and maintain a public Copyright Office agent, receive and promptly triage notices, locate and disable access to the relevant content it controls, notify uploaders, accept legally specified counter-notices, and support the 10–14-business-day restoration branch. The product must first resolve which entity controls each hosted file, relay copy, and feed rendering; this report does not determine statutory status.
2. Keep notice intake separate from a definitive “infringement” label. Make the process usable for posts, screenshots, recordings, documents, videos, and files; request enough information to identify both the protected work and the exact content/location complained of. Avoid treating a hash match, signature, C2PA manifest, or absence of credentials as a legal merits test.
3. Design removal scope honestly: distinguish hiding in the Truth-Talk client, disabling a URL or hosted object under an operator’s control, requesting deletion from a relay, and actual global deletion (which cannot be guaranteed in the Nostr model). Preserve a route to reverse a notice-based removal in response to a valid counter-notice when the responsible provider is following Section 512(g).
4. If operating the covered service, include a published, maintained designated-agent route and an informed, reasonably implemented repeat-infringer policy in the service design; protect the personal contact details counter-notices require and restrict their use to process needs. Confirm operational staffing and retention/security controls before enabling user uploads or asserting compliance.
5. Treat anti-catfishing as a distinct trust-and-safety program. Cryptographic receipt integrity and content provenance may help evidence integrity/history; they cannot, without independent identity proofing and additional checks, establish who appears in a 4Reels clip or that a claimant owns rights to included music, footage, screenshots, or other incorporated works.

These are product implications of the cited sources, not legal advice or a legal determination about Truth-Talk, the client, relays, or any individual upload.
